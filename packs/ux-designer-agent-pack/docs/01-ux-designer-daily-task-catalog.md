# UX Designer — Complete Daily Task Catalog

This catalog defines 207 tasks that a UX Designer Agent and its specialist sub-agents may perform. Not every task occurs every day; the supervisor selects a bounded set based on the project, evidence, risk, and workflow stage.

## Work intake and prioritization

1. Open assigned Jira stories, design requests, defects, and discovery items.
2. Read business goals, acceptance criteria, product context, comments, and linked dependencies.
3. Review existing Figma files, prototypes, decision logs, prior research, support themes, analytics, and implementation history.
4. Identify the target project, product area, environment, design system, user roles, and delivery date.
5. Check whether the request is discovery, concept design, detailed design, design-system work, usability validation, or design QA.
6. Identify missing context, assumptions, contradictions, and decisions that require stakeholder clarification.
7. Estimate UX effort, research needs, review needs, and approval dependencies.
8. Prioritize work based on user impact, business risk, accessibility, release timing, and evidence gaps.
9. Update the design work status and communicate blockers.
## Problem framing and discovery

10. Translate feature requests into user problems, jobs, outcomes, and measurable behavior changes.
11. Separate stakeholder requests from verified user needs.
12. Map known facts, assumptions, hypotheses, risks, and evidence gaps.
13. Identify affected personas, roles, devices, workflows, channels, and operational teams.
14. Review competitive or analogous experiences using approved public sources.
15. Identify legal, privacy, security, accessibility, localization, and brand constraints.
16. Create or update a design brief.
17. Define in-scope and out-of-scope experience boundaries.
18. Define success measures and guardrail measures.
19. Run stakeholder interviews or workshops when approved.
20. Document design principles for the initiative.
## User research planning

21. Define the decision the research must support.
22. Write research questions that are neutral and answerable.
23. Choose qualitative, quantitative, or mixed methods.
24. Define participant criteria and exclusion criteria without unnecessary sensitive attributes.
25. Estimate sample needs based on method and decision risk.
26. Draft screeners, consent language, interview guides, test tasks, surveys, or diary prompts.
27. Plan recording, note-taking, transcription, redaction, retention, and deletion.
28. Identify participant burden and safety risks.
29. Create a research repository structure and evidence naming convention.
30. Request approvals for recruitment, incentives, consent, recording, or external tools.
## Research execution

31. Recruit participants through approved channels.
32. Confirm consent before each session and before recording.
33. Conduct stakeholder, user, customer, or support interviews.
34. Conduct contextual inquiry and workflow observation.
35. Moderate usability tests using neutral prompts.
36. Run card sorts, tree tests, concept tests, preference tests, or diary studies.
37. Capture timestamped observations, quotes, behaviors, errors, and workarounds.
38. Avoid leading participants or teaching the intended answer.
39. Monitor participant distress, privacy, and the right to stop.
40. Store raw recordings and identifiers only in approved repositories.
41. Create redacted transcripts or notes for agent analysis.
## Research synthesis and insights

42. Code observations and statements using a consistent scheme.
43. Cluster evidence into themes without hiding contradictory evidence.
44. Separate observation, interpretation, inference, and recommendation.
45. Rate evidence strength, frequency, impact, and confidence.
46. Triangulate interviews, usability sessions, analytics, support, and operational data.
47. Create insight statements tied to user and business outcomes.
48. Identify opportunity areas and design principles.
49. Update research repository tags and links.
50. Create findings summaries for product, engineering, QA, support, and leadership audiences.
51. Document limitations, bias, missing segments, and unresolved questions.
## Personas, jobs, journeys, and service design

52. Create or update evidence-backed personas and jobs-to-be-done.
53. Label proto-personas and unsupported assumptions clearly.
54. Map current-state and future-state journeys.
55. Identify stages, actions, thoughts, emotions, channels, pain points, and moments of truth.
56. Map accessibility and inclusion barriers across the journey.
57. Create service blueprints covering frontstage, backstage, systems, policies, and handoffs.
58. Identify operational failure points and recovery needs.
59. Map ecosystem actors, ownership, and dependencies.
60. Prioritize opportunities by evidence, impact, feasibility, and risk.
## Information architecture

61. Inventory current content, screens, navigation, and duplicated concepts.
62. Design taxonomy, hierarchy, labels, navigation, and sitemap.
63. Design search, filtering, sorting, facets, and information scent.
64. Run card sorting and tree testing when needed.
65. Define content types, metadata, relationships, and governance.
66. Design breadcrumbs, wayfinding, section navigation, and cross-links.
67. Validate IA with representative tasks and roles.
68. Document migration or compatibility impacts for existing URLs and workflows.
## Interaction design

69. Create user flows, task flows, state diagrams, and permission paths.
70. Design happy paths, alternate paths, edge cases, empty states, errors, and recovery.
71. Design forms, validation, defaults, progressive disclosure, and confirmation behavior.
72. Design loading, latency, offline, retry, interrupted, and partial-success states.
73. Design complex tables, filters, bulk actions, pagination, and selection behavior.
74. Design role-based experiences and unauthorized states.
75. Specify keyboard order, focus behavior, shortcuts, and announcements.
76. Design responsive behavior for desktop, tablet, and mobile.
77. Specify microinteractions, transitions, feedback, and motion alternatives.
78. Review feasibility with frontend, backend, QA, and database teams.
## Visual and UI design

79. Apply hierarchy, grid, spacing, typography, color, iconography, and imagery.
80. Reuse approved components and tokens before creating new patterns.
81. Design all component variants and interaction states.
82. Create responsive compositions at required breakpoints.
83. Design dark mode, high-contrast, or theme variants where supported.
84. Check visual consistency across the product and related projects.
85. Design accessible data visualizations with non-color encodings.
86. Prepare production-safe icons, illustrations, images, and asset guidance.
87. Review brand alignment and license metadata.
88. Create polished high-fidelity designs.
## Content design and localization

89. Write clear labels, instructions, helper text, errors, empty states, confirmations, and notifications.
90. Use product terminology and plain language consistently.
91. Make errors specific, actionable, and non-blaming.
92. Design content hierarchy and scanning behavior.
93. Review tone for stressful, destructive, security, or financial actions.
94. Avoid idioms and culturally narrow assumptions.
95. Prepare copy for translation and text expansion.
96. Review date, time, number, currency, address, and name formats.
97. Review RTL layout and mirrored interaction risks.
98. Maintain terminology and content decision logs.
## Design systems

99. Audit existing components, variants, tokens, and implementation coverage.
100. Define component anatomy, API, states, content rules, responsive behavior, and accessibility.
101. Create or update semantic design tokens.
102. Map Figma components and variables to production components and tokens.
103. Review Storybook or code implementation before proposing duplicates.
104. Create component contribution and review proposals.
105. Plan deprecation and migration for replaced components.
106. Maintain library naming, file architecture, branching, versioning, and release notes.
107. Measure adoption, duplication, design debt, and accessibility debt.
108. Obtain approval before publishing shared-library changes.
## Prototyping

109. Create low-fidelity wireframes for structure and flow.
110. Create clickable prototypes for primary and alternate paths.
111. Use realistic content and data rather than placeholder-only screens.
112. Include errors, empty states, permissions, loading, and recovery.
113. Build responsive prototypes for critical breakpoints.
114. Create motion prototypes only when motion supports comprehension.
115. Instrument prototypes for task testing.
116. Version prototypes and preserve links to the decision and story.
117. Run internal walkthroughs before participant studies.
## Accessibility and inclusive design

118. Map designs to applicable WCAG 2.2 success criteria.
119. Use native semantic patterns and ARIA APG guidance when designing custom widgets.
120. Check text and non-text contrast.
121. Check target sizes, spacing, focus visibility, and focus order.
122. Specify keyboard behavior for every interactive element.
123. Specify screen-reader names, roles, states, properties, and live announcements.
124. Design zoom, reflow, text resizing, and orientation behavior.
125. Avoid color-only, motion-only, audio-only, or spatial-only instructions.
126. Provide reduced-motion alternatives.
127. Review cognitive load, reading complexity, error prevention, and recovery.
128. Include disabled users in research where relevant and approved.
129. Document accessibility acceptance criteria and known limitations.
## Usability validation

130. Define realistic tasks and success criteria.
131. Prepare moderated or unmoderated test protocols.
132. Pilot the study before external sessions.
133. Measure task completion, errors, assistance, time, confidence, and satisfaction.
134. Capture behaviors and quotes without over-weighting preferences.
135. Rate findings by severity, frequency, impact, confidence, and affected users.
136. Identify root causes rather than only surface symptoms.
137. Recommend design changes tied to evidence.
138. Retest high-risk or substantially revised flows.
139. Communicate limitations and unresolved risk.
## Analytics and experimentation

140. Review event taxonomies and data quality before interpreting analytics.
141. Analyze funnels, paths, adoption, retention, abandonment, and cohort differences.
142. Use privacy thresholds and avoid exposing individual user behavior.
143. Write testable hypotheses and predicted behavior changes.
144. Define primary metrics, secondary metrics, and guardrails.
145. Create experiment measurement plans with product and analytics partners.
146. Review experiment results with confidence intervals and segment checks.
147. Combine analytics with qualitative evidence.
148. Do not launch experiments without product, analytics, and required privacy approval.
## Developer handoff

149. Prepare implementation-ready flows, screens, states, tokens, assets, and content.
150. Map designs to existing components and code references.
151. Specify component variants, responsive rules, and interaction behavior.
152. Specify accessibility semantics, keyboard behavior, focus, announcements, and contrast.
153. Write design acceptance criteria linked to Jira.
154. Identify API, data, permission, and loading dependencies.
155. Document unresolved decisions and acceptable implementation flexibility.
156. Conduct handoff walkthroughs with frontend, backend, QA, and product.
157. Answer implementation questions and update the decision log.
## Design QA and release support

158. Confirm the intended build, branch, environment, feature flags, browser, and viewport.
159. Compare implementation with approved Figma and design decisions.
160. Review layout, spacing, typography, color, icons, content, and component states.
161. Review responsive behavior and content overflow.
162. Review keyboard, focus, screen reader, contrast, and target-size behavior.
163. Review errors, loading, empty, permission, offline, and recovery states.
164. Classify differences as implementation defect, acceptable adaptation, or design update.
165. Create evidence-backed Jira defects or design QA comments after approval.
166. Retest fixes and document accepted residual risk.
167. Provide design readiness input without replacing QA or release authority.
## Design operations and improvement

168. Maintain Figma project structure, libraries, templates, naming, branching, and permissions.
169. Maintain research repository taxonomy and retention.
170. Track design requests, capacity, cycle time, blocked time, and review queues.
171. Facilitate design critique and record decisions.
172. Track design debt, accessibility debt, and component duplication.
173. Create reusable checklists and standards.
174. Review escaped usability issues and support incidents.
175. Measure design-system adoption and handoff quality.
176. Mentor designers and share research or design-system knowledge.
177. Improve collaboration rituals across product, engineering, QA, data, and support.
## Communication and governance

178. Attend product planning, refinement, stand-up, design critique, research review, and release-readiness meetings when relevant.
179. Publish approved design summaries, findings, decisions, risks, and next actions to Jira, Confluence, or Teams.
180. Escalate critical accessibility, privacy, usability, or brand risks immediately.
181. Maintain traceability from requirement to evidence, design decision, prototype, handoff, implementation, and validation.
182. Coordinate with Product Managers, Business Analysts, Angular developers, Java/Python developers, QA, DB architects, DevOps, security, support, and compliance.
183. Record decision owners, dates, alternatives, consequences, and follow-up actions.
184. Protect participant confidentiality in presentations and stakeholder discussions.
185. Request explicit approval before external participant contact, shared library publication, public sharing, or experiment launch.
## Quality and continuous improvement

186. Review whether designs solve the stated user problem rather than only matching requested screens.
187. Review evidence freshness and whether target user segments are represented.
188. Check consistency with established product patterns and cross-project experiences.
189. Track assumptions that remain unvalidated after release.
190. Review production feedback and analytics after release using privacy-safe aggregates.
191. Add recurring issues to design-system guidance, checklists, or research plans.
192. Archive superseded designs while preserving decision history and links.
193. Remove obsolete prototypes, duplicated assets, and stale library branches according to retention policy.
194. Evaluate whether AI-generated research or design output is supported by human evidence and deterministic checks.
195. Document uncertainty instead of inventing user needs, metrics, or research findings.
