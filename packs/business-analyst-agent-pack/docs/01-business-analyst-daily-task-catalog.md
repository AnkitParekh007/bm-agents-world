# Business Analyst Agent — Complete Daily Task Catalog

This catalog contains **220 daily and periodic tasks**. Not every task runs every day; the supervisor selects tasks according to the request, lifecycle stage, risk, project, and approved scope.

## Work Intake and Scope

1. Open assigned Jira epics, stories, bugs, requests, and change items.
2. Read the business objective, requested outcome, scope, priority, and target date.
3. Review linked Confluence pages, policies, decisions, designs, and previous analyses.
4. Identify the requesting stakeholder, accountable decision owner, and affected product area.
5. Confirm the project, environment, release, process, system, and business unit in scope.
6. Separate stated solution requests from the underlying business need.
7. Identify exclusions, constraints, assumptions, dependencies, and unresolved questions.
8. Determine the required business-analysis approach and deliverables.
9. Estimate analysis effort and sequencing.
10. Create or update the analysis work plan.

## Stakeholder Analysis and Collaboration

11. Identify primary, secondary, operational, regulatory, technical, and external stakeholders.
12. Map stakeholder influence, interest, decision rights, and information needs.
13. Create or update a RACI or responsibility model.
14. Identify conflicting objectives, incentives, terminology, and constraints.
15. Plan stakeholder engagement and validation checkpoints.
16. Prepare interview guides and workshop agendas.
17. Facilitate requirement, process, scope, and decision workshops.
18. Record decisions, actions, owners, due dates, and unresolved topics.
19. Confirm elicitation results with participants.
20. Escalate missing decision owners or unavailable subject-matter experts.

## Business Need and Strategy Analysis

21. Define the business problem or opportunity in neutral language.
22. Identify affected customers, users, partners, employees, and operational teams.
23. Analyze root causes rather than treating symptoms as requirements.
24. Define measurable business outcomes and success indicators.
25. Map the request to organizational objectives and capabilities.
26. Document current limitations, risks, costs, and consequences of no change.
27. Identify policy, contractual, regulatory, or strategic constraints.
28. Define current state, future state, and transition gaps.
29. Compare process, policy, training, configuration, and technology response options.
30. Prepare an evidence-backed problem and opportunity brief.

## Elicitation

31. Select elicitation techniques appropriate to uncertainty and stakeholder availability.
32. Review existing documents, tickets, screens, reports, logs, and procedures.
33. Conduct structured stakeholder interviews.
34. Facilitate collaborative workshops and example-mapping sessions.
35. Observe real work and capture workarounds, handoffs, and exceptions.
36. Use prototypes, wireframes, or process models to elicit feedback.
37. Design surveys only when the target population and decision use are clear.
38. Capture business language without prematurely translating it into implementation.
39. Separate confirmed facts, stakeholder opinions, assumptions, and hypotheses.
40. Confirm elicitation notes and resolve material disagreements.

## Current-State Process Analysis

41. Define process boundaries, triggers, inputs, outputs, customers, and suppliers.
42. Create SIPOC and high-level process views.
43. Model actors, systems, activities, decisions, events, and handoffs.
44. Document normal, exception, escalation, cancellation, and recovery paths.
45. Identify manual steps, duplicate entry, rework, queues, delays, and bottlenecks.
46. Identify control points, approvals, segregation-of-duties, and audit evidence.
47. Capture cycle time, wait time, volume, failure rate, and service-level evidence.
48. Identify undocumented workarounds and shadow processes.
49. Validate current-state models with operational stakeholders.
50. Baseline current-state measures before proposing change.

## Future-State and Process Improvement

51. Define future-state principles and target outcomes.
52. Remove unnecessary steps and duplicate approvals where policy permits.
53. Clarify ownership, decision rights, and handoff expectations.
54. Design future-state normal and exception paths.
55. Evaluate automation, standardization, self-service, and policy-change opportunities.
56. Preserve mandatory controls and evidence requirements.
57. Assess new failure modes and operational dependencies.
58. Define transition states and coexistence processes.
59. Validate future-state feasibility with business and technical stakeholders.
60. Document process changes, benefits, risks, and adoption needs.

## Requirements Analysis and Specification

61. Classify business, stakeholder, solution, transition, functional, and nonfunctional requirements.
62. Assign stable requirement identifiers and owners.
63. Decompose broad needs into atomic and testable requirements.
64. Remove duplicate, contradictory, and obsolete requirements.
65. Replace vague wording with measurable conditions and examples.
66. Document rationale, source, priority, assumptions, and dependencies.
67. Model states, events, decisions, data, interfaces, and user interactions where useful.
68. Validate necessity, feasibility, value, consistency, completeness, and testability.
69. Baseline approved requirements and preserve version history.
70. Maintain a controlled requirements catalog.

## Business Rules and Decision Logic

71. Identify policies, regulations, calculations, thresholds, eligibility, and validation rules.
72. Separate business rules from process steps and implementation details.
73. Write atomic rule statements with owners and effective dates.
74. Create decision tables for multi-condition outcomes.
75. Create decision trees for complex branching logic.
76. Document rule precedence, conflicts, overrides, and exceptions.
77. Provide positive, negative, and boundary examples.
78. Trace rules to policy sources, requirements, stories, and tests.
79. Validate rule interpretation with accountable business owners.
80. Track proposed, approved, superseded, and retired rules.

## Data and Reporting Requirements

81. Define business entities, concepts, terms, and relationships.
82. Create or update the business glossary and data dictionary.
83. Identify data owners, stewards, producers, consumers, and systems of record.
84. Define required fields, formats, validation, defaults, and derivations.
85. Define data quality, completeness, timeliness, and reconciliation rules.
86. Define retention, archival, deletion, residency, privacy, and consent needs.
87. Define reporting measures, dimensions, filters, frequencies, and audiences.
88. Identify data migration, mapping, transformation, and historical-load needs.
89. Identify lineage and audit-trail requirements.
90. Validate data requirements with database, analytics, privacy, and business owners.

## Interface and Integration Requirements

91. Identify systems, partners, users, devices, files, APIs, events, and batch interfaces.
92. Define interface purpose, ownership, direction, frequency, and criticality.
93. Document payload concepts and business-level field meaning.
94. Define triggers, acknowledgements, errors, retries, duplicates, and timeouts.
95. Define ordering, idempotency, correlation, and reconciliation expectations.
96. Identify availability windows and dependency assumptions.
97. Define versioning and backward-compatibility business needs.
98. Document privacy, security, audit, and nonrepudiation expectations.
99. Map integration failures to operational handling and support procedures.
100. Validate integration requirements with architecture and engineering teams.

## Nonfunctional and Compliance Requirements

101. Quantify expected volumes, concurrency, latency, throughput, and growth.
102. Define availability, recovery, continuity, and resilience needs.
103. Define authentication, authorization, confidentiality, and audit expectations.
104. Define privacy, consent, minimization, retention, and residency needs.
105. Define accessibility and inclusive-use requirements.
106. Define localization, timezone, currency, and language requirements.
107. Define supportability, monitoring, alerting, and operational reporting needs.
108. Define legal, regulatory, contractual, and policy controls.
109. Define usability and error-recovery expectations.
110. Trace every nonfunctional requirement to evidence, owner, validation, and acceptance method.

## User Stories, Use Cases, and Acceptance

111. Convert approved needs into implementation-ready user stories where appropriate.
112. Split oversized stories by workflow, rule, data, role, or outcome.
113. Write use cases for complex actor-system interactions.
114. Define actors, triggers, preconditions, postconditions, and guarantees.
115. Document happy paths, alternate paths, exceptions, and recovery.
116. Write clear acceptance criteria in rule, checklist, or Given-When-Then form.
117. Add examples for boundary values, permissions, invalid data, and failure conditions.
118. Link stories to requirements, designs, rules, dependencies, and tests.
119. Check stories against the definition of ready.
120. Resolve developer, QA, UX, architecture, and product-owner questions.

## Prioritization and Scope Management

121. Apply the approved prioritization method to requirements and stories.
122. Separate mandatory, high-value, optional, deferred, and out-of-scope needs.
123. Document value, urgency, risk, dependency, and effort inputs.
124. Expose uncertainty and sensitivity in prioritization recommendations.
125. Identify the minimum viable or minimum marketable scope.
126. Assess scope changes against outcomes, dependencies, cost, risk, and release.
127. Prevent silent scope expansion through untracked requirements.
128. Maintain deferred requirements and rationale.
129. Prepare decision requests for unresolved scope conflicts.
130. Update baselines only after authorized decisions.

## Traceability and Lifecycle Management

131. Trace business goals to needs, requirements, stories, designs, code, tests, releases, and outcomes.
132. Detect orphan requirements and unimplemented acceptance criteria.
133. Detect delivery work without an approved requirement or decision source.
134. Maintain source, owner, status, version, and approval metadata.
135. Compare requirement baselines and summarize material changes.
136. Assess downstream impacts before approving requirement changes.
137. Preserve superseded requirements and decision history.
138. Report coverage gaps by requirement type and risk.
139. Support audit and compliance evidence requests.
140. Close or retire requirements after verified outcome and retention checks.

## Change Impact and Readiness

141. Analyze impacts to people, roles, responsibilities, and workload.
142. Analyze impacts to processes, procedures, controls, and policies.
143. Analyze impacts to applications, integrations, data, reports, and infrastructure.
144. Analyze impacts to support, operations, training, communications, and vendors.
145. Identify transition data, coexistence, migration, and cutover needs.
146. Assess readiness by stakeholder group and location.
147. Identify adoption barriers and resistance risks.
148. Define training, communication, documentation, and support actions.
149. Create an impact register with owners and due dates.
150. Review readiness before implementation, pilot, and release.

## UAT Planning and Business Acceptance

151. Define UAT objectives, scope, participants, roles, and decision authority.
152. Define UAT entry and exit criteria.
153. Create business scenarios tied to requirements and real workflows.
154. Prepare privacy-safe UAT data and account needs.
155. Confirm environment, integrations, reports, and support availability.
156. Define evidence capture, defect triage, retest, and waiver procedures.
157. Coordinate UAT schedule and participant readiness.
158. Track execution, blocked scenarios, defects, and unresolved decisions.
159. Prepare a business-acceptance recommendation with residual limitations.
160. Ensure final acceptance or waiver is made by an accountable human.

## Solution Evaluation and Benefits

162. Evaluate whether the solution addresses the original business need.
163. Measure adoption, usage, quality, efficiency, risk, and customer outcomes.
164. Identify solution limitations, process gaps, and unintended effects.
165. Distinguish product defects from process, training, data, or policy issues.
166. Assess whether expected benefits are realized.
167. Identify corrective, optimization, decommissioning, or further-discovery options.
168. Conduct post-implementation reviews.
169. Update lessons learned and reusable patterns.
170. Recommend requirement, process, or roadmap changes for human decision.

## Quality Assurance of Analysis

## Governance, Security, and Ethics

201. Use only authorized sources for the declared purpose.
202. Apply minimum-necessary access and data minimization.
203. Keep raw credentials and secret values outside model context.
204. Protect employee, customer, participant, commercial, and regulated data.
205. Do not infer protected characteristics or fabricate stakeholder views.
206. Do not make legal, compliance, pricing, contractual, release, or budget commitments.
207. Require payload-bound approval for external publication and material requirement changes.
208. Prevent production mutations and unrestricted database queries.
209. Record tool calls, evidence, decisions, approvals, and outputs immutably.
210. Apply retention and deletion policies to temporary analysis context.
